AI creates 16 new viruses that never existed in nature after learning DNA’s pattern from 9 trillion nucleotides — experts warn such applications are way ahead of necessary guardrails (1 minute read)

Researchers at Stanford and the Arc Institute trained a genomic AI model called Evo on over 9 trillion nucleotides, then used it to design 285 complete viral genomes. When the sequences were chemically synthesized, 16 assembled into functioning bacteriophages capable of infecting bacteria and reproducing. It is the first time an AI has generated viable viruses from scratch rather than editing existing genomes, and experts warn the capability is running ahead of biosecurity guardrails.

LLMs reward expertise (5 minute read)

The author argues that the most important prompting skill is domain expertise, not prompt-craft. Because everyone queries the same models, novices and "skilled prompters" get similar results on generic tasks, but expertise changes the conversation itself. The author points to Terence Tao's ChatGPT exchange on the Jacobian Conjecture counterexample: Tao signals expertise to shunt the model into an expert register, pushes back on wrong-looking output, and directs the path himself rather than following the model's suggestions.

This AI Just Created Viruses Not Found in Nature (5 minute read)

Researchers used the Evo AI model from the Arc Institute to design bacteriophage genomes from scratch, producing viable viruses with no natural counterpart. The generated phages successfully infected and killed E. coli bacteria in the lab, marking the first time a generative model has designed functional genomes at the scale of a complete organism. The work sharpens biosecurity concerns, since the same approach that designs beneficial phages could in principle design pathogens.

Atlassian Rovo Can Be Tricked Into Sending Jira and Confluence Data to Attackers (1 minute read)

Two security firms independently showed that Atlassian's Rovo assistant can be tricked into gathering Jira and Confluence data a signed-in user can access and exfiltrating it to an outside server. PromptArmor hid instructions in an uploaded file that Rovo reads, while Varonis abused the rovoChatPrompt URL parameter to preload attacker instructions from a single click. Varonis's link-based flaw, dubbed RovoBlast, was fixed server-side on July 8; the content-borne path has no patch and is mitigated only by scoping which apps and groups can use Rovo.
