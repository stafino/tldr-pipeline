How Organizations Use AI: Evidence from ChatGPT (1 minute read)

This paper links ChatGPT Enterprise account records to worker roles, task types, and public-company financials through March 2026, analyzing over 17 million messages across more than 1,500 organizations at the six-month adoption mark. Usage is growing through both new firm adoption and rising intensity among existing users, and it concentrates in larger, more R&D- and SG&A-intensive companies. Adoption spans job functions and seniority, with the heaviest use among early-career workers.

Nvidia’s Risky Business (1 minute read)

Ben Thompson draws a parallel between Nvidia and Jay Cooke, whose retail-bond financing of the Northern Pacific Railway collapsed into the Panic of 1873. The argument is that Nvidia is increasingly underwriting demand for its own chips through circular deals and vendor financing, taking on the same downstream risk that felled Cooke when capital needs proved endless and credit tightened. The historical rhyme is the warning: infrastructure booms funded by their own supplier tend to end badly.

Toward Meaningful Transparency for AI Chatbots: Disclosing Persuasive Intent Reduces Persuasion (1 minute read)

A preregistered experiment with 1,500 UK adults tested whether disclosing an AI chatbot's persuasive intent blunts its influence. Simply revealing that users were talking to an AI made no difference: attitudes shifted 13.1 points versus 12.6 in the control. Adding the chatbot's persuasive intent and instructions cut the shift to 6.3 points and made participants judge the campaign less acceptable. The authors argue regulation must address what a system is trying to do, not just what it is.

What sort of maths are LLMs good at? (1 minute read)

Timothy Gowers examines what kinds of mathematics current LLMs are actually good at, prompted by OpenAI's recent claim to have solved ten major open problems, including the first construction of a non-sofic group and superexponential growth for a multicolour Ramsey number. He notes that the most famous solved problems have mostly involved finding counterexamples rather than constructing proofs, and treats the piece as a snapshot of capabilities in early August 2026 that he expects to age quickly.

Prompt Injections for Defense (1 minute read)

Researchers at Tracebit found that embedding prompt injections alongside passwords and cryptographic keys in AWS often shut down attacks from AI hacking agents. The planted prompts order the attacking LLM to perform a guardrail-forbidden action, such as detailing anthrax production or referencing Tank Man, at which point the model refuses to continue and abandons its task. The technique, called context bombing, only works against agents with guardrails, so locally run models without them will leave attackers unaffected.

AI for Military Support (1 minute read)

A study tested a high-fidelity replica of a military AI targeting decision-support system on 2,015 Israeli military personnel across two experiments. Contrary to fears of automation bias, the authors found strong algorithmic aversion, especially in high-collateral-damage scenarios. Adding explainable AI features reduced that aversion and prompted more deliberate evaluation, suggesting trust in military AI shifts with operational stakes, individual predispositions, and how the interface presents information.

Chinese Censorship Is Leaking Into Answers From American AI (1 minute read)

The article attributes this to Chinese-origin training data seeping into Western models, and reports that the major labs have done little to correct the behavior.

Why Does CLAUDE.md Keep Growing? Catastrophic Remembering in Agentic Coding (1 minute read)

This paper argues that agentic coding instruction files like CLAUDE.md grow without bound because appending is cheap while safe deletion costs O(2^|D|) once an instruction's rationale is lost. The authors call this catastrophic remembering, the inverse of catastrophic forgetting. Across 247,694 instruction lifetimes in 1,867 repositories, prompts more than tripled (+226%) and older instructions grew less likely to be deleted. Adding comments that encode latent reasoning removed 99.3% of excess instructions and improved real-world instruction-following by up to 23.1%.

Compression is prediction (1 minute read)

This post argues that lossless compression and language modeling are fundamentally the same problem: both work by predicting the next piece of data and spending fewer bits on what they expect. It walks through compression basics, from run-length encoding to the transform, model, and entropy-coder stages of tools like gzip and Brotli, to show that a better predictor is a better compressor.

Total Recall at What Cost? Benchmarking the Serving Cost of Agentic Memory Systems (1 minute read)

This paper benchmarks the serving cost of three agentic memory systems (Mem0, Hindsight, and Mastra Observational Memory) against rolling-window and full-transcript baselines across two backbones and conversations up to 400 turns, pairing each cost measurement with accuracy on 665 LoCoMo questions. Memory cost cannot be predicted from conversation length alone, break-even against resending the full transcript ranges from the first tens of turns to never within 400 turns, and no system wins on both cost and accuracy.

An Agentic Workflow for Legacy HPC Modernization: Converting the Two-Electron-Integral Core of GAMESS (1 minute read)

This paper presents an agentic workflow that converts the two-electron-integral core of GAMESS, a 48-year-old quantum-chemistry package, from fixed-form Fortran 77 to free-form Fortran 2008. Three prompt-specialized Claude Code agents modernized 56,448 lines across 225 subroutines, using bit-for-bit reproduction of the standard test suite's energies as the merge criterion, and produced zero chemistry-relevant differences across 612 test runs.

Who Thinks Best Depends on How Long You Let Them: Budget-Dependent Rankings in LLM Evaluation (1 minute read)

This paper varies the token generation budget from 64 to 4,096 across four models and three reasoning benchmarks, showing that LLM rankings reverse depending on how much output each model is allowed. The authors find 3-19% of items get less accurate with more budget, measure model complementarity up to 27.8pp at constrained budgets, and argue for budget-conditioned evaluation protocols.

Claim-Level Reliability Assessment for Efficient Test-Time Reasoning (1 minute read)

This paper introduces Claim-Level Reliability Assessment (CLR), a training-free method that reallocates test-time compute from extra solution sampling to targeted verification by condensing each reasoning trace into a few decision-critical claims and refuting them. On GPT-OSS-20B/CMIMC25, CLR exceeds pass@1 by 27.15 percentage points and raises self-consistency accuracy from 77.50% to 82.19% while using 37% fewer tokens.

The Next Challenge for Agentic Cybersecurity: A Realistic, Contamination-Free Reverse Engineering Benchmark (1 minute read)

SRE-Bench is the first contamination-free reverse engineering benchmark, built from scratch by RE experts over 5,000 hours to test whether AI agents can analyze binaries without source code. It comprises 19 private, real-world-scale programs and 1,572 graded tasks, and the strongest model, GPT-5.6-sol, scored 61.4% per instance while fully solving only 31.5% of instances.

AI Guardrail Survival under Single-Cycle Agentic Self-Summarization (1 minute read)

This paper examines how a safety rule is lost when a long-running agent compacts its context in a single summarization cycle, finding that a rule surviving as text often no longer governs behavior. On behavioral replay, degraded rule residues led models to perform prohibited actions 34 to 57 points more often than intact rules, so presence-based audits that only check for a rule's text give false assurance.

Long-Horizon AI Research for Grothendieck Constant: A Case Study in Human-AI Mathematical Collaboration (1 minute read)

This paper presents a case study of using an AI research system to tighten the best known bounds on the Grothendieck constant K_G, which measures the gap between combinatorial problems and their continuous relaxations. The authors report that the system produced insights domain experts deemed novel, and they detail its strengths, weaknesses, and the conditions that let it reach breakthrough results.
