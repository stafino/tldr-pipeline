News & Trends

Type designers have created a free font that "poisons" AI (1 minute read)

ShieldFont, a free open-source web font from Brazilian studio Seneda & Abrucio and Copenhagen foundry Playtype, renders as clean, legible text to humans while feeding AI scrapers altered wording through OpenType glyph substitution. Built on Playtype's Optik in six weights, the v18 release swaps high-frequency English nouns in the source HTML, corrupting 55.8% of shielded passages in controlled tests while keeping sentences grammatically coherent enough to pass AI quality filters.

For 100 years no-one noticed it, but this rebrand finally gives Gunnersbury Museum a reason to be found (1 minute read)

Wiedemann Lampe rebranded London's Gunnersbury Park and Museum with a modular identity that treats the shared site as two entities that can split or combine: a tree symbol in green for the park, a gabled building in purple for the museum, and a yellow lockup when they speak as one. Color carries the logic into wayfinding, letting either the museum or park promote its own programming without dragging the other along.

Saarinen's Dulles terminal set to be enveloped by Trump-backed airport (1 minute read)

The Department of Transportation released conceptual renderings for a Dulles International Airport overhaul that builds atop Eero Saarinen's 1962 terminal, developed with the Metropolitan Washington Airports Authority and United Airlines and backed by President Trump. The plan encloses one of American modernism's most recognizable structures inside a larger complex, raising the preservation question of whether Saarinen's sweeping concrete roofline survives as a landmark or a facade.

UX-Context Design: Using UX Knowledge to Inform AI-Generated Design (5 minute read)

Nielsen Norman Group argues that as product managers, engineers, and AI tools increasingly produce designs, the deliverable that matters most is context: the users, domain knowledge, design standards, and research findings a model can see when it generates an interface. The piece reframes personas, journey maps, and research reports as inputs written for AI consumption rather than human interpretation, since generic output is mostly a symptom of missing context.

Jakob’s law: How to apply it as AI collapses surfaces into one chat box (3 minute read)

The author argues that Jakob's Law now works in reverse: as assistants like Claude and ChatGPT pull tasks into a single conversational surface, the shared asking-box pattern means learning one AI tool teaches you all of them, which accelerates adoption without any onboarding. The takeaway is that design systems and their semantic layer matter more than ever, since explicit, predictable conventions are what let users move confidently across an increasingly uniform set of interfaces.

Rethinking friction in an AI world (3 minute read)

The author argues that friction reduction has become UX orthodoxy, but with AI removing effort by default, designers should distinguish the three friction types (interaction, cognitive, emotional) and decide where a pause actually serves the user. The strongest cases for keeping friction are high-stakes moments like financial confirmations or destructive actions, where a deliberate speed bump builds trust rather than eroding it.

The accessibility paradox (2 minute read)

The same accessibility work that helps humans—semantic HTML, alt text, meaningful link labels—also makes pages trivially interpretable for AI crawlers and training pipelines, as with LAION-5B pairing Common Crawl images to their accessibility alt text. The author argues WCAG has quietly become infrastructure for the AI industry, absorbing creative work without consent, attribution, or compensation, and leaves the harder question of what designers do about it unresolved.

The UX of Contrast: Lessons from Indika’s Game Design (2 minute read)

The author analyzes Indika, a 2024 narrative game, to introduce "PX Contrast": a design approach where gameplay, sound, UI, and progression deliberately pull against each other to sustain conflicting player interpretations at once. The piece extends contrast beyond its usual role as a visual attention tool, arguing that intentional contradiction across an experience can carry emotional meaning that consistency would flatten.

The AI Aesthetic (1 minute read)

The author catalogs the visual idioms emerging from the AI moment, from sparkle icons and shimmering "thinking" text to the tiny, thin app icons that clash with native macOS grain, plus beige palettes, orange accents, and serif type. Some patterns like streaming text stay locked to chat, but others such as shimmer-as-async are already spreading into general software, making this a useful field guide to which conventions will outlast the fad.

How design leaders influence decisions without being in the room (5 minute read)

A designer argues that self-explanatory screens no longer carry product reviews, since redesigns are subtler and AI mockups now look finished enough to fool non-designers, so senior designers must annotate their own work with a three-part frame: decision, hypothesis, outcome. The essay is sharpest on research readouts, where a FigJam board of 200 stickies proves a workshop happened without telling a VP what was actually learned.

Digital Tools, Human Expression: The Visual Identity Behind Config 2026 (5 minute read)

Figma's Brand Studio detailed the visual identity behind Config 2026, built around three glyph styles (sketchy, generative, crisp) plus AI-generated textures produced with custom Figma Make tools to codify lo-fi effects like scribbly linework, blurred gradients, and dithering. The system's premise is that AI-assisted work should look wonky and idiosyncratic yet clearly programmatic, a useful case study for teams trying to keep a human point of view while designing with generative tools.

Designing Fast and Slow (5 minute read)

Andy Budd argues that designers default to Kahneman's System 2, treating product decisions like chess moves that are hard to undo, while business partners play poker, placing many fast bets and expecting to lose some. He contends that reflexive slowness gets design branded an organizational handbrake, and that knowing when a decision is genuinely reversible is what lets designers pick their System 2 battles.

Decoy Font protects messages from being read by AI (1 minute read)

Mixfont released Decoy Font, a typeface that hides two messages in each letter: a foreground outline that LLMs read from an image's pixels and a darker background form that humans see by squinting or shifting focus. Available as a web app for encoding specific hidden text or a downloadable TTF, it fooled GPT-5.6 Sol and Gemini 3.5 into reading "sleep early" as "binge shows".
