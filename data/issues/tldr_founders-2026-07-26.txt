Uber-Gründer sammelt 1,7 Milliarden Dollar für sein Robotik-Startup ein (1 minute read)

Travis Kalanick raised $1.7B for Atoms, his industrial robotics startup, in a round led by Andreessen Horowitz that brings Ben Horowitz onto the board and pulls in Bain Capital, Fifth Wall, and Uber itself. Atoms is the renamed CloudKitchens holding company, now folded together with automation startup Pronto, and Kalanick is aiming it at a "chassis for robots" that makes manufacturing, logistics, and mining more efficient. Prentis, new AI lab co-founded by Reid Hoffman, Mark Pincus in talks to raise $100M (1 minute read)

Prentis, a new computer-use AI lab co-founded by Reid Hoffman and Mark Pincus, is in talks to raise $100M at a $1B valuation just three months after launching. It has already signed up to $50M in contracts by charging 20% of the savings its agents realize, and claims its 32B Hive model beats GPT-5.4 and Claude Opus 4.6 on two computer-use benchmarks at roughly a tenth of the cost per task. The bet is that automating routine office workflows overtakes coding as AI's biggest use case, in a market already crowded with Anthropic, OpenAI, and Thinking Machines.

OpenAI’s early backer Khosla Ventures targets largest fund ever at $5.5B with most going to early-stage AI startups (1 minute read)

Khosla Ventures is raising up to $5.5B across its newest funds, the largest haul in its 20-year history and close to 40% above its last vintage. More than half, $3B, is aimed at seed and early-stage AI startups, the riskiest stage and the one where the firm made its name writing OpenAI's first outside check. The bet doubles down on early AI at a moment when most capital is chasing later, safer rounds, and Khosla's recent deals in legal AI, defense, and robotics show where that money is headed.

Candid Health, which develops tech and AI agents to automate medical billing and claims processing workflows, raised a $120M Series D led by Sixth Street Growth (Lily Mae Lazarus/Fortune) (1 minute read)

Candid Health raised a $120M Series D led by Sixth Street Growth to automate medical billing and claims processing with AI agents. Revenue-cycle work is one of healthcare's largest and most tedious back-office costs, which makes it a natural target for agents that handle repetitive, rules-heavy workflows end to end. The bet is that billing and claims are where AI first pays for itself in healthcare, well before diagnosis or care, because the work is structured, high-volume, and tied directly to how fast providers get paid.

America’s Open-Model Paradox (1 minute read)

Chinese open models now sit inside most of the American AI stack. Qwen's share of new open-model fine-tunes and adaptations climbed from 1% in January 2024 to 69% by February 2026, and the majority of US AI startups appear to use Chinese open weights somewhere in their pipeline. The deeper problem is legal asymmetry: a Western lab can lawfully post-train on a Chinese open model, but distilling from GPT or Claude outputs is prohibited, so every American frontier advance becomes a teacher for Chinese labs and hands them a recurring structural edge.

Bond market anxiety is growing over AI capex budgets (1 minute read)

Bond investors are getting nervous about how much the AI buildout costs, and it's showing up in credit spreads for Google, Amazon, and Meta as lenders demand more to hold their debt. Mizuho estimates the hyperscalers will collectively spend more on capex than they generate in free cash flow by next year, with rising power costs and $100 oil pushing budgets higher. Oracle is the pressure gauge, its 5-year CDS back at a multi-year high and its credit rating cut to BBB-, one notch above junk, as the market treats it as a proxy for AI debt fears.

AMD and Anthropic Sign Major Chips-and-Investment Deal (5 minute read)

AMD and Anthropic signed a chips-and-investment deal that ties one of the industry's largest AI labs to AMD silicon alongside its existing Nvidia and Google TPU capacity. The pact gives AMD a marquee frontier customer to validate its Instinct accelerators against Nvidia's near-monopoly on training compute, while Anthropic diversifies the hardware it depends on to keep scaling. For founders building on Anthropic's models, the signal is that compute supply is fanning out across more vendors, which over time should ease the capacity crunch that has capped access and kept inference prices high.

Nvidia locks down memory supply from SK Hynix as part of $500 billion AI deal (1 minute read)

Nvidia secured up to $500B in high-bandwidth memory supply from SK Hynix over several years, locking down the scarcest input for its GPUs as the AI boom drives a global HBM shortage. The deal pairs with a 2-gigawatt data center buildout, a $200B Samsung-Broadcom memory pact, and a $1B Nvidia stake in Korean cloud firm Naver. The signal for founders is that AI infrastructure has moved past a handful of US hyperscalers, with foreign governments and conglomerates now bidding for the same compute and memory you need.

OpenAI Raised Its Infrastructure Plans to $750 Billion (5 minute read)

OpenAI raised its infrastructure budget to $750 billion through 2030, about 25% more than it projected earlier this year, even as its flagship Stargate project appears to have stalled. The first move is Project Camellia, a $20 billion campus near Savannah drawing at least 3.2 gigawatts from Georgia Power, roughly a third of the utility's newly approved capacity. Most of that power will come from natural gas, more than doubling Georgia Power's gas fleet, which puts OpenAI's compute buildout squarely on the same fossil-fuel dependence and local-grid strain now shaping where every frontier lab can physically expand.

OpenAI unveils Presence, a new platform that lets enterprises launch and manage realtime voice agents and chatbots (5 minute read)

OpenAI announced Presence, an enterprise platform for deploying and managing realtime voice agents and chatbots that answer questions, access company systems, take approved actions, and escalate to humans under company-defined policies. It bundles the guardrails, system connections, evaluations, and update processes that founders usually have to stitch together themselves. It ships through a limited GA with OpenAI deployment engineers, is not self-service, and runs OpenAI models at the core while letting customers connect third-party models for guardrails and tools.

The most valuable thing Apple built in the 2000s wasn't the iPod (5 minute read)

iTunes, not the iPod, was Apple's most consequential build of the 2000s. Its 99¢ per-track store launched in April 2003 with about 200,000 songs, hit its six-month sales forecast in six days, and carried more than 70% of legal digital downloads within three years. It worked because Apple stacked strategic levers rather than just selling songs online, and in the process it unbundled the album and set up the streaming and playlist economy we live in now.
