Spain’s Multiverse Computing hits unicorn status after raising €500 million Series C at €1.5 billion valuation (1 minute read)

The bet is that efficient, self-hosted AI beats the assumption that powerful models require expensive hyperscaler infrastructure, and €701M in total funding is behind it.

Prentis, new AI lab co-founded by Reid Hoffman, Mark Pincus in talks to raise $100M (1 minute read)

Prentis, a new computer-use AI lab co-founded by Reid Hoffman and Mark Pincus, is in talks to raise $100M at a $1B valuation just three months after launching. It has already signed up to $50M in contracts by charging 20% of the savings its agents realize, and claims its 32B Hive model beats GPT-5.4 and Claude Opus 4.6 on two computer-use benchmarks at roughly a tenth of the cost per task. The bet is that automating routine office workflows overtakes coding as AI's biggest use case, in a market already crowded with Anthropic, OpenAI, and Thinking Machines.

BAE Systems' energy spinout Nuclear Turbines emerges from stealth with £15M raise (1 minute read)

A BAE Systems spinout, Nuclear Turbines, emerged from stealth with a £15M round led by IQ Capital to build compact modular reactors it claims can generate electricity at one-fifth the cost of today's nuclear plants. The Manchester startup swaps expensive steam turbines for high-temperature jet-engine turbine tech paired with a novel reactor, small enough to sit behind the meter at data centers and industrial sites. The bet lands as the UK, saddled with some of Europe's highest electricity prices, pushes a new nuclear buildout and a "radical reset" of its regulation.

Quantum computing startup Multiverse bags $570M at $2.3B valuation: The story behind its 96x growth in 4 years (1 minute read)

Multiverse Computing raised $570M at a $2.3B valuation, five times its Series B mark and bringing total funding to $800M. The San Sebastián startup grew revenue 96 times year-over-year in Q1 2026 by selling CompactifAI, a compression engine that uses tensor networks from quantum physics to shrink open-source models by 80% to 95% and run them four to twelve times faster at half the cost. The bet is that efficient, sovereign AI at the edge and in the data center is where the demand is heading, and the capital is following the compression layer rather than the frontier labs.

AI vendors are switching from subscriptions to consumption pricing. AI PCs are the hedge. (1 minute read)

AI vendors are dropping the flat-rate subscriptions that seeded early adoption and moving to token consumption and outcome-based pricing, which means enterprise AI bills are about to climb and stop being predictable. The hedge is local compute: AI PCs with neural processing units now run small models on-device for the routine high-volume work, summarization, drafting, code completion, and data extraction, at zero marginal cost per query. For heavy users, the payback on a $1,500 machine is months, so the real question is no longer cloud versus local but which tasks belong where.

Nvidia and OpenAI in talks for up to $250 billion backstop to fund AI infrastructure plans (1 minute read)

OpenAI is in talks with Nvidia for a backstop of up to $250B that would let it raise construction and lease debt for a 10-gigawatt data center in Ohio on the strength of Nvidia's credit. The campus alone could top $500B, and the chips inside are being negotiated separately. The structure shows how the AI buildout now runs on chipmakers underwriting their biggest customer's balance sheet, a circular financing loop that ties Nvidia's fortunes ever tighter to OpenAI ahead of its filed IPO.

Nvidia may guarantee $250bn so OpenAI can afford the data centre that will house its chips (1 minute read)

Nvidia is in talks to guarantee roughly $250B in financing so OpenAI can lease a 10-gigawatt data center campus in southern Ohio, according to the Wall Street Journal. The backstop exists because OpenAI lacks an investment-grade credit rating, so Nvidia's balance sheet stands in for one and lenders price the debt against the chipmaker rather than the borrower. With a separate $350B under discussion for the chips themselves, the whole project could top $500B, and the structure means a chip supplier is now underwriting its own customer's ability to keep buying chips.

Antares raises $470M to build nuclear reactors for the US military (1 minute read)

Antares raised $470M in a Series C led by Paradigm and Caffeinated Capital to build small modular reactors for US military bases, split as $370M equity and $100M debt. The round is part of a nuclear funding surge driven by AI data center power demand, following X-energy's $1B IPO and nine-figure rounds for Radiant, Standard Nuclear, and Last Energy. The catch is timing: SMR economics depend on mass-manufacturing savings that historically take a decade to arrive, and no startup has reached that scale, so first deployments at military installations aren't planned until 2028.

OpenAI Raised Its Infrastructure Plans to $750 Billion (5 minute read)

OpenAI raised its infrastructure budget to $750 billion through 2030, about 25% more than it projected earlier this year, even as its flagship Stargate project appears to have stalled. The first move is Project Camellia, a $20 billion campus near Savannah drawing at least 3.2 gigawatts from Georgia Power, roughly a third of the utility's newly approved capacity. Most of that power will come from natural gas, more than doubling Georgia Power's gas fleet, which puts OpenAI's compute buildout squarely on the same fossil-fuel dependence and local-grid strain now shaping where every frontier lab can physically expand.

OpenAI unveils Presence, a new platform that lets enterprises launch and manage realtime voice agents and chatbots (5 minute read)

OpenAI announced Presence, an enterprise platform for deploying and managing realtime voice agents and chatbots that answer questions, access company systems, take approved actions, and escalate to humans under company-defined policies. It bundles the guardrails, system connections, evaluations, and update processes that founders usually have to stitch together themselves. The most valuable thing Apple built in the 2000s wasn't the iPod (5 minute read)

iTunes, not the iPod, was Apple's most consequential build of the 2000s. Its 99¢ per-track store launched in April 2003 with about 200,000 songs, hit its six-month sales forecast in six days, and carried more than 70% of legal digital downloads within three years. It worked because Apple stacked strategic levers rather than just selling songs online, and in the process it unbundled the album and set up the streaming and playlist economy we live in now.
