Analyzing data from Silicon Valley ventures and founders prosecuted for fraud (1 minute read)

Researchers pulled data on Silicon Valley ventures and the founders who were later prosecuted for fraud, and the pattern is uncomfortable for the industry. The traits that get founders celebrated, relentless optimism, hype, and a willingness to bend the story ahead of the facts, are the same ones that show up in the ones who crossed into fraud. The line between visionary and fraudster is drawn mostly after the fact, by whether the bet paid off. For founders, the takeaway is that "fake it till you make it" is a legal risk, not just a cultural one.

Apple Earnings, More on Amazon’s Earnings (1 minute read)

Apple's earnings are being held back by chip shortages, not the memory constraints most analysts had assumed, which caps how much the current cycle can grow. On the Amazon side, Andy Jassy's read of the market points to demand still outrunning available supply. The takeaway across both is that hardware capacity, not customer appetite, is the binding constraint on the biggest tech names right now.

Cambridge Aerospace's valuation leaps to $3.4BN as raises $300M (1 minute read)

Cambridge Aerospace raised $300M at a $3.4bn valuation, up from $1.3bn in April, less than two years after the counter-drone startup was founded. The round, led by DFJ Growth with Lux, Accel, Lakestar, and Elad Gil, funds manufacturing scale-up behind existing contracts, including a UK Ministry of Defence deal for its Skyhammer drone interceptor. European defence tech is now attracting US growth capital at software-round velocity, with valuations more than doubling in a single quarter as governments race to field low-cost interceptors against drone and missile threats.

Revolut clinches French banking licence (1 minute read)

Revolut secured a French banking licence from the ACPR and the ECB, adding a second EU license on top of its Lithuanian one as it moves to serve around 30M Western European customers. The move follows ECB restrictions last year over how fast Revolut approved new products, so a locally supervised licence buys regulatory cover in its fastest-growing region. Revolut is investing more than €1B and hiring 600-plus people in Western Europe, opening a Paris HQ next year, all against a reported $115B secondary valuation and a pending US banking application.

Smart Analytics: Chinese humanoid robot makers were 97%+ of global shipments in H1 2026, reaching ~19,100 unit shipments globally, up from 5,100 in H1 2025 (Saritha Rai/Bloomberg) (1 minute read)

Chinese manufacturers accounted for more than 97% of global humanoid robot shipments in the first half of 2026. Global shipments hit roughly 19,100 units, up from 5,100 a year earlier, nearly a fourfold jump in twelve months. The supply chain for humanoids is consolidating in China before the category has a mass market, which means Western robotics startups are increasingly building on hardware and components sourced from the same firms they hope to compete with.

Embattled hedge fund Situational Awareness invests $400M in chip startup Source Foundry (1 minute read)

Situational Awareness put $400M into chip startup Source Foundry, a Stanford-founded bet on faster, cheaper manufacturing, bringing its total stake to $500M. The AI-focused hedge fund made the move weeks after selling most of its public portfolio to Citadel, cutting assets under management from $20B to $10B as AI infrastructure stocks slid. Founder Leopold Aschenbrenner, a former OpenAI researcher who launched the fund in 2024 with no trading experience, is doubling down on private compute infrastructure even as his public book unwinds.

A profile of Founders Pledge, which has recorded $4B in pledges from founders so far in 2026, as the AI industry now accounts for a third of its lifetime total (Joel Khalili/Wired) (1 minute read)

Founders Pledge has recorded $4B in commitments from startup founders so far in 2026, and AI now accounts for roughly a third of its lifetime pledged total. Tel Aviv-based QuantHealth, a provider of AI clinical trial simulation software, raised a $45M Series B led by Qumra Capital, taking its total funding to ~$70M (Cailey Gleeson/Fierce Healthcare) (1 minute read)

QuantHealth raised a $45M Series B led by Qumra Capital, pushing the Tel Aviv startup's total funding to roughly $70M. The company builds AI software that simulates clinical trials before they run, letting drug developers test protocols, dosing, and patient response in silico rather than burning years and budget on trials that fail. The raise lands as pharma pours money into anything that shortens the drug-development cycle, and simulation is one of the few AI use cases where a single avoided trial failure can justify the entire spend.

Edgify raises $9M to expand its edge AI platform (1 minute read)

Edgify raised $9M in a Series A+ from Rank Ventures and Mangrove Capital Partners, bringing total funding to $25M for its edge AI platform that turns self-checkouts, cameras, scales, and point-of-sale systems into a coordinated intelligence layer. Devices process and learn locally instead of shipping raw data to the cloud, cutting latency and infrastructure cost while keeping customer data in the store. The company started in grocery loss prevention and is now moving into convenience stores, quick-service restaurants, and distribution centers, betting the same legacy-hardware problem repeats across every industry where fleets of devices meet the physical world.

UK air defence startup Cambridge Aerospace hits $3.4bn valuation in new fundraise (5 minute read)

Cambridge Aerospace raised $300M at a $3.4bn valuation, led by DFJ Growth, just months after a $200M Series B. The two-year-old British startup makes Skyhammer, a drone and low-speed missile interceptor already under contract with the UK armed forces and tested in Jordan. Defense capital is flowing to air defense specifically because modern conflicts in Ukraine and Iran exposed the gap, and investors want systems that are faster to build and cheaper to field, which is pulling frontier venture money into hardware that ships to governments rather than software that ships to enterprises.

China Humanoid Makers Hold 97% of Global Shipments, Report Says (1 minute read)

China's humanoid robot makers shipped more than 97% of the world's units in the first half of 2026, according to new industry data. The lead traces to a stacked domestic supply chain, state backing, and cheaper components that US rivals can't yet match on cost. For founders building in robotics or hardware-adjacent AI, the competitive frontier is already forming overseas, and the bill of materials, not the demo, is where the early advantage is being won.

OpenAI just bought a slide deck startup. It is assembling an office suite one acqui-hire at a time. (1 minute read)

OpenAI acquired NextSlide, a roughly one-year-old startup that turned prompts, notes, and documents into finished editable presentations, with the team moving onto ChatGPT. The deal fits a clear pattern: buy a small team with a working product, retire the brand, and slot the capability into ChatGPT alongside documents, canvas, and file handling that already add up to an office suite. The awkward part is who that suite competes with, because the product NextSlide replaced is PowerPoint, and Microsoft is OpenAI's largest backer, which is why EU and UK regulators are now watching the relationship.

Atlassian Just Deleted Loom’s Free Creator Seats. They Were Also Loom’s Distribution. (1 minute read)

Atlassian killed Loom's free Creator Lite seats, the viewer-and-commenter role inside paying workspaces, and auto-converts every one of those users to a full paid Creator seat on the integration date. A workspace with 10 recorders and 90 watchers used to pay for 10 and now pays for 100, but the watchers were never dead weight. They were the funnel, because one recording pulled in 20 viewers and a few of them started recording too, which is how Loom reached 25 million users. Charging for your top of funnel collects revenue and quietly shuts off the loop that created it.

The playbook for building high talent density teams | Adam Ward, Head of Talent at Cursor (1 minute read)

The traditional recruiting funnel that Cursor's head of talent calls the "funnel of doom" optimizes for volume and lands you mediocre hires, because the best people are never in the applicant pool to begin with. His playbook inverts it: scope the role precisely, map the specific people who could do it, then pursue them relentlessly rather than posting and praying. The biggest mistake founders make is hiring their first recruiter to run the funnel instead of running that targeted pursuit.
