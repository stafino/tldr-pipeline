AI Cloud Provider Lambda Taps Loans for Nvidia-Tied Chip Deal (1 minute read)

Lambda, the Nvidia-backed AI cloud provider, is selling a leveraged loan to finance a chip purchase, pushing the AI buildout into the riskier corners of the debt market. Cloud providers used to fund GPU fleets with equity and Nvidia's own capital, but the numbers have grown too large for that, so they're now borrowing against the hardware itself. When infrastructure companies start leaning on leveraged loans to buy chips that depreciate fast, the financing risk of the AI boom stops being an equity story and becomes a credit one.

Tel Aviv- and San Francisco-based Corma, which develops AI models for defensive cybersecurity, emerges from stealth with a $60M seed led by Sequoia Capital (Emily Forlini/Fortune) (1 minute read)

Corma emerged from stealth with a $60M seed led by Sequoia Capital, one of the largest seed rounds in cybersecurity. The Tel Aviv- and San Francisco-based startup builds AI models aimed at defense rather than offense, betting that the same model capabilities powering automated attacks can be turned toward detecting and blocking them. The round size signals where security spend is heading, as investors position for a market in which AI-driven threats and AI-driven defenses escalate against each other.

How Mailchimp Went From $1B+ ARR to … Shrinking Inside Intuit. A Death Spiral in the Age of AI? (1 minute read)

Intuit paid roughly $12B for Mailchimp in 2021, and five years later it is publishing its growth rate with the asset stripped out. On its May 2026 call, Intuit's Online Ecosystem grew 19%, or 22% without Mailchimp, and management confirmed the unit's revenue is now declining while announcing a 17% workforce cut and about $300M in restructuring charges. The decline started before AI, but the agent era made it fatal: the entry point for new email moved from a marketer signing up for a UI to a developer or model provisioning it by API, and Mailchimp is not in that room.

Global New Unicorn Counts In The First Half Of 2026 Have Already Surpassed 2025’s Totals (1 minute read)

195 companies joined the Crunchbase Unicorn Board in the first half of 2026, already topping the 193 minted in all of 2025 and the highest pace since late 2022. Robotics and AI neolabs led the cohort, with the US minting 110 of the new unicorns and China surging to 38, up from 10 a year earlier. The new class added roughly $440B in value on $80B raised, headlined by DeepSeek at $50B, and 19 of them doubled their valuation in fast follow-on rounds inside six months.

The Biggest Consequence Of An AI IPO Isn’t The IPO Itself. It’s What Happens Afterward. (1 minute read)

The real story of the coming AI IPOs isn't how OpenAI, Anthropic, Databricks, or Stripe trade after the bell. It's the distributions that hit limited partners once those exits clear, returning capital that venture has been starved of for years and freeing endowments, pension funds, and sovereign wealth to recommit. That liquidity is likely to concentrate rather than spread: the 10 largest US venture funds took nearly a third of all capital raised in 2025, while first-time fund formation fell to its lowest in over a decade. A sustained wave of listings won't lift the whole ecosystem evenly. It will hand the biggest managers the largest share.

Whatnot raises $545M from ICONIQ, Lightspeed and Avra to fuel live commerce expansion (1 minute read)

Whatnot raised $545M in a Series G at a $20B valuation, nearly doubling its worth in under a year, led by ICONIQ, Lightspeed, and Avra. In a year when venture capital has narrowed almost entirely to AI, a live-streaming marketplace for Funko Pops, Pokémon cards, and sneakers pulled in over half a billion dollars, with gross merchandise volume already topping last year's full total halfway through 2026. The global live commerce market is projected to hit $230B in 2026 and grow toward $2.55T by 2033, with Europe and North America the fastest-growing regions Whatnot is now targeting.

The Deluge of AI Debt Has Big Names Seeking New Strategies to Avoid Selloffs (1 minute read)

The AI buildout is now being funded with a wave of debt, and the biggest arrangers are engineering the deals specifically to keep fast-money investors from triggering a selloff. BlackRock structured its Meta data-center financing to lock in patient capital rather than let hedge funds churn the paper. The signal for founders is that the capital behind the AI infrastructure race is getting more fragile and more actively managed, which means the terms, not just the demand, will decide how long the spending holds up.

SEC Exempts Data-Center Bonds From Key Securitization Rules (1 minute read)

The SEC exempted data-center bonds from key securitization rules, making it easier for owners to sell asset-backed securities. The move opens a wider debt channel for tech firms scrambling to fund the AI buildout, whose compute capex has outrun what cash flow and equity alone can cover. Expect more data-center ABS issuance ahead, which turns physical infrastructure into a financeable asset class and shifts some of the AI capital race onto the bond market, where the cost and pace of borrowing start to shape who can keep building.

Anthropic, Macquarie, and Singapore's GIC form Theseus Infrastructure to develop AI computing sites; Anthropic commits to cover consumer electricity price hikes (Mark Bergen/Bloomberg) (1 minute read)

Anthropic, Macquarie, and Singapore's GIC formed a joint venture called Theseus Infrastructure to build AI computing sites, and Anthropic committed to cover the consumer electricity price hikes those data centers cause. The pledge is a tell: power, not chips, is becoming the binding constraint on AI buildout, and the political cost of straining local grids is now large enough that a frontier lab will absorb it directly. For founders, it signals that compute expansion is moving into project-finance territory, where energy contracts and community goodwill matter as much as model quality.

Anthropic, Macquarie and GIC Form Venture for AI Data Centers (1 minute read)

Anthropic partnered with Macquarie Asset Management and Singapore's GIC to build and finance data centers dedicated to Claude. The deal pulls infrastructure capital from an asset manager and a sovereign wealth fund rather than the labs' own balance sheets, spreading the cost of the compute buildout across outside investors. For founders, it signals that frontier capacity is increasingly a project-finance game, and the labs racing to lock up power and hardware now are the ones who set pricing and availability for everyone building on top of them.

Can Agents Use a Computer Yet? We've Got the Data (1 minute read)

Computer-using agents have crossed from demo to production, holding up on narrow, repeatable back-office work like updating systems of record, moving data through portals, and processing tickets where no clean API exists. They still break when the work drifts off the runbook, and for cases where caching is intractable the cost math doesn't yet close. But the frontier is Business Process Outsourcing, and the structural edge is hard to argue with: an agent that works 24/7 and scales to demand is starting to beat humans clicking through legacy systems by hand.

Sila lands $1.4B Pentagon loan as militaries demand more batteries (1 minute read)

The Pentagon just handed Sila a $1.4B loan to scale silicon-carbon battery material, part of a broader push to buy anode supply that doesn't run through China. Graphite anodes dominate today's lithium-ion cells and their supply chain is Chinese-controlled, so Sila's Moses Lake factory is one of the few tariff-free sources, with silicon promising 20% to 40% more energy density. The signal for founders: defense capital is now flowing into the unglamorous materials layer, and government loans plus contracts with Mercedes and Panasonic are becoming a viable path to scale hard-tech manufacturing.

Unitree’s Shanghai IPO 5,526 Times Subscribed by Retail Buyers (1 minute read)

Unitree's Shanghai IPO was oversubscribed 5,526 times by retail investors, one of the frothiest debuts China has seen for a hardware maker. The demand signals that public markets now treat humanoid robotics the way they treated EVs and solar a decade ago, betting on a category before the unit economics are proven. For founders in physical AI, the read is that capital is racing ahead of revenue, and the winners will be the ones who can convert that enthusiasm into shipped units before the subsidy-and-hype cycle turns.
