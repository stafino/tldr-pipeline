General Catalyst leads $1.1B round into 2-month-old River AI (1 minute read)

General Catalyst and AMP PBC led a $1.1B seed/Series A into River AI, a two-month-old startup from xAI co-founder Igor Babuschkin, with Nvidia, AMD Ventures, Y Combinator, and Temasek joining. River sells a neocloud API for post-training open models with RL and LoRA, claiming enterprises can finish a complex reinforcement learning run in 15 to 20 minutes with no infra team, at two to four times the cost savings of closed alternatives. The bet is that enterprises want to own their models, and the round shows how much capital is chasing that thesis.

Nvidia’s Risky Business (1 minute read)

Nvidia’s position now rhymes with Jay Cooke’s railroad bonds, the innovation that funded Northern Pacific by selling debt to retail investors until the buyers ran out and triggered the Panic of 1873. The parallel is the funding structure, not the technology: when a single supplier underwrites the demand for its own product, capital needs stay endless and the whole thing holds only as long as fresh money keeps arriving. The risk for founders building on top is that AI compute pricing rests on financing that can tighten fast, the way credit did in 1873.

AI code-testing startup Blacksmith’s valuation jumps almost 10x in less than a year (1 minute read)

Blacksmith raised a $45M Series B led by Peak XV that values it at $550M, up from $60M less than a year ago, on the bet that testing is the next bottleneck once AI makes code cheap to write. Customers jumped from about 700 to more than 5,000, including Mercury and Supabase, and revenue climbed from a $10M run rate to tens of millions. The catch is a crowded field, with GitHub Actions, Cursor, and validation baked directly into Codex and Claude Code all chasing the same shift.

Beijing forced Meta to unwind its $2 billion acquisition of Manus, and the general-purpose AI agent startup now says it will soon resume operating as an independent company. China's regulator ordered the withdrawal in April over foreign-investment rules, and the country has since tightened export controls on cross-border tech deals as it competes with the US for AI talent, hardware, and data. The lesson for founders is that a signed acquisition no longer clears the board when the target's home government treats the technology as strategic.

Mapping the AI economy (1 minute read)

Stripe's payments data shows AI companies are going global fast, with the 100 largest reaching 120 markets on average by their third year and drawing 48% of revenue from outside their home markets. Pylon’s Founders at SaaStr AI Day: A 1,000-Person Support Team Deflected 50% of Its Tickets. Headcount Didn’t Change. (1 minute read)

Deflection rate is the wrong number for AI in customer support, and Pylon's founders point to a company of roughly 1,000 support people that automated resolution on around 50% of tickets and still changed no headcount. Their argument is that support bought full-replacement agents when the fastest-growing AI companies run human-plus-AI augmentation instead, especially in B2B where tickets carry more context and relationship. The metric that actually moves is whether the job becomes unrecognizable, with tickets arriving already investigated. Pylon's beta customers report 70% fewer escalations at one and 64.5% faster first response at another.

A Founder’s Guide to the Secondary Market in 2026 (1 minute read)

Secondaries have become the main way founders get liquidity before an IPO, but the market is far more selective than the headlines suggest. In early 2026, institutional holders tried to move roughly $600M of OpenAI stock and found no buyers, while Anthropic closed a $5.5B tender at a $380B valuation with demand running hot, all in the same quarter. Around 80% of secondary money goes to about 20 companies, so treat this as a real liquidity option only if you are one of the few names buyers actually want.

Why deeptech founders are dictating term sheets in 2026 — and how you can secure a fair deal (5 minute read)

UK venture has split into a barbell, and deeptech and AI founders are on the winning end, commanding founder-friendly terms while every other sector gets squeezed. Larger deals now make up 31% of all term sheets, up from 26% in 2024, as investors pile capital into fewer high-growth companies. The leverage is real but conditional: it only shows up once you have more than one term sheet, so the move is to manufacture competition among investors and use it to hold board control and cut the day-to-day decisions that need their sign-off.

Peter Steinberger on building in the agent era (1 minute read)

Peter Steinberger spent 13 years building PSPDFKit, sold it, swore off founding again, then agents got good enough to pull him back as the creator of OpenClaw. His lesson is that the job has shifted from writing code to engineering the pipeline that lets an agent write, review, and test it end to end. He credits his edge to having run a company before, which taught him to design the loop rather than just prompt for output.

Researchers Built a Fake Crypto Startup and Hired Three Suspected North Korean IT Workers (1 minute read)

Security researchers built a fake DeFi startup called Ballena Azul, advertised developer jobs, and hired three suspected North Korean operatives, recording every work VM they issued. The onboarding paperwork is the tell hiring teams can actually use: mismatched addresses across states, a New York license belonging to someone else, and ID images processed through Google Gemini and carrying its SynthID watermark. Nobody exploited anything. Each operative cleared an interview, signed a contract, and got authorized access to source code, which is exactly why the hiring pipeline is now the attack surface.

Can software factories actually work? (1 minute read)

Software factories automate the coding pipeline so agents can pull tickets, ship PRs, and deploy through CI/CD while humans read less and less of the output. The debate is whether the "lights-off" version, where agents write everything and humans review nothing, can hold up at scale, and HumanLayer's Dex Horthy argues no harness ever makes it work. Most teams sit in the middle: PostHog's agents write about 70% of PRs, but humans still skim 80% of them.
