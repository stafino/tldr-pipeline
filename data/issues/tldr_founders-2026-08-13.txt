Nvidia’s Risky Business (1 minute read)

Nvidia’s position now rhymes with Jay Cooke’s railroad bonds, the innovation that funded Northern Pacific by selling debt to retail investors until the buyers ran out and triggered the Panic of 1873. The parallel is the funding structure, not the technology: when a single supplier underwrites the demand for its own product, capital needs stay endless and the whole thing holds only as long as fresh money keeps arriving. The risk for founders building on top is that AI compute pricing rests on financing that can tighten fast, the way credit did in 1873.

How Organizations Use AI: Evidence from ChatGPT (1 minute read)

Enterprise AI adoption is real but wildly uneven across firms. A new study linking ChatGPT Enterprise records to worker roles and public-company financials tracked over 1,500 organizations and 17 million messages through March 2026. Usage is concentrated in larger, more valuable firms that spend heavily on R&D and SG&A, and within those firms it spans every job function and seniority level, running hottest among early-career workers. The tasks cover the full range of knowledge work: writing, technical work, communication, and synthesis. The gap isn't whether firms adopt, but how fast, broad, and deliberate that adoption is.

Mapping the AI economy (1 minute read)

Stripe's payments data shows AI companies are going global fast, with the 100 largest reaching 120 markets on average by their third year and drawing 48% of revenue from outside their home markets. The most interesting expansion targets aren't just the high-GDP giants but markets where AI spend runs disproportionately high, like India, Mexico, Poland, and the UAE. Presence alone doesn't convert, though, so the companies building real international revenue localize the product, payment methods, and marketing rather than assuming a global launch sells itself.

Josh Kushner's Thrive Holdings raised more than $2bn at a $12bn valuation to buy the unglamorous businesses AI is coming for, rather than sell them tools. B2B For Physical Products Is Crushing It: Shopify +34%, Toast +23%, Samsara +30% (1 minute read)

While AI batters public software, the vendors selling into the physical world are accelerating. Shopify grew revenue 34%, Toast 23%, and Samsara 30% last quarter, roughly two to three times the 13% median public B2B company and Salesforce's own 13%. The pricing model is part of it, since none charge primarily per seat, but the bigger reason is that their customers' end products are restaurants, shipped goods, and truck fleets, not the knowledge work AI is eating. Revenue tracks throughput, so these companies grow when customers sell, open, or move more.

Pylon’s Founders at SaaStr AI Day: A 1,000-Person Support Team Deflected 50% of Its Tickets. Headcount Didn’t Change. (1 minute read)

Deflection rate is the wrong number for AI in customer support, and Pylon's founders point to a company of roughly 1,000 support people that automated resolution on around 50% of tickets and still changed no headcount. Their argument is that support bought full-replacement agents when the fastest-growing AI companies run human-plus-AI augmentation instead, especially in B2B where tickets carry more context and relationship. The metric that actually moves is whether the job becomes unrecognizable, with tickets arriving already investigated. Pylon's beta customers report 70% fewer escalations at one and 64.5% faster first response at another.

A Founder’s Guide to the Secondary Market in 2026 (1 minute read)

Secondaries have become the main way founders get liquidity before an IPO, but the market is far more selective than the headlines suggest. In early 2026, institutional holders tried to move roughly $600M of OpenAI stock and found no buyers, while Anthropic closed a $5.5B tender at a $380B valuation with demand running hot, all in the same quarter. Around 80% of secondary money goes to about 20 companies, so treat this as a real liquidity option only if you are one of the few names buyers actually want.

Why deeptech founders are dictating term sheets in 2026 — and how you can secure a fair deal (5 minute read)

UK venture has split into a barbell, and deeptech and AI founders are on the winning end, commanding founder-friendly terms while every other sector gets squeezed. Larger deals now make up 31% of all term sheets, up from 26% in 2024, as investors pile capital into fewer high-growth companies. The leverage is real but conditional: it only shows up once you have more than one term sheet, so the move is to manufacture competition among investors and use it to hold board control and cut the day-to-day decisions that need their sign-off.

Can software factories actually work? (1 minute read)

Software factories automate the coding pipeline so agents can pull tickets, ship PRs, and deploy through CI/CD while humans read less and less of the output. The debate is whether the "lights-off" version, where agents write everything and humans review nothing, can hold up at scale, and HumanLayer's Dex Horthy argues no harness ever makes it work. Most teams sit in the middle: PostHog's agents write about 70% of PRs, but humans still skim 80% of them.
